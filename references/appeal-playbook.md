## Principles for every context

- **Answer the allegation, not the detector.** The reviewer wants to know whether the writer did the work. Lead with how the work was made.
- **Be brief.** A first message fits on one screen. The packet carries the detail.
- **Ask for specifics in writing.** Which tool, what score, which passages, which policy, and what process and deadlines apply. Writers cannot answer an allegation they have not seen.
- **Be cooperative.** Offer to walk through the drafts and discuss the work. Willingness to be questioned is persuasive.
- **Follow the actual procedure.** Find the policy (syllabus, student handbook, honor code, employee handbook, contract, platform terms) and meet its deadlines. Missing a deadline can matter more than the evidence.
- **Keep it in the writer's voice.** A defense of authorship written in polished, generic prose undercuts itself. Outline the points and have the writer phrase them, or lightly edit their own draft.
- **Never accuse the reviewer.** Assume they are following a tool they were told to trust. Make it easy for them to change their mind.

## Structure of a first response

1. **Statement.** One sentence: "I wrote this essay myself."
2. **Request.** Ask for the report, the flagged passages and the policy, if not already provided.
3. **Evidence summary.** Two to four sentences describing the process with dates, for example "I drafted it in Google Docs between 2 and 14 October; the version history shows it growing from my outline to the final draft over nine sessions. I've attached screenshots and my notes."
4. **Offer.** "I'm happy to meet, show the version history and talk through how I developed the argument."
5. **Context on detectors, optional.** One or two sentences with a source from `detector-limits.md`, framed around the reviewer's own guidance where possible.
6. **Close.** Thank them; restate availability.

## Context notes

**Instructor or teacher (first contact).** Often the fastest route to resolution. Keep it informal and short. Offer a meeting in office hours. Many instructors drop the matter once they see version history. Do not copy in administrators on the first message unless the policy requires it.

**Formal academic-integrity process.** Read the procedure carefully: rights to see the evidence, to respond in writing, to bring an advisor, to appeal. Submit the packet in the required format. Stick to facts and dates. Refer to the institution's own guidance on AI detection if it exists. Suggest the student contact the student advocate, ombudsperson or student union, especially if penalties could affect progression, funding or visa status.

**Employer.** Keep it factual and professional. Show work files, version history and any messages about the work. If company policy permits some AI tools, say exactly what was and was not used. For performance or disciplinary processes, suggest the employee check their rights with HR policy, a union representative or an employment advisor.

**Client or freelance platform.** Respond quickly and without defensiveness. Offer a screen-share walkthrough of the drafting process and the version history. Point to the contract: most do not define "AI-free" or say which detector governs. If a platform dispute is involved, follow its dispute process and keep all communication on the platform. Proposing a short supervised writing sample on a related topic can resolve doubt quickly.

**Publisher, editor or journal.** Provide drafts, research notes and correspondence. Editors respond well to evidence of reporting or research (interview notes, recordings, source lists). Ask what their AI policy actually prohibits.

## Phrases that help and phrases that hurt

Helps:
- "I wrote this myself, and I'm glad to show you how."
- "Could you share the report and the passages that were flagged?"
- "My version history shows the essay developing from my outline over two weeks."
- "Turnitin's own guidance says the score shouldn't be the sole basis for action, so I'd like the chance to show my drafts."

Hurts:
- "AI detectors are garbage / everyone knows they don't work." (argues with the reviewer)
- "You have no proof." (combative, and invites them to look for some)
- Long lists of studies. (reads as a campaign, not an answer)
- Rewriting the flagged work and resubmitting it "to show it's human". (looks like tampering)
- Any claim the evidence does not support.

## Example outline (for the writer to put in their own words)

To: [instructor]
Subject: [Assignment name]: AI detection flag

- I wrote this essay myself and want to resolve this quickly.
- Could you send me the AI report and the parts that were flagged?
- I drafted it in Google Docs from [date] to [date]. The version history shows it growing from my outline; screenshots and my notes are attached.
- I'd be glad to meet and walk you through the drafts and my sources.
- [Optional] Turnitin's guidance says the AI score shouldn't be the only basis for a decision, which is why I wanted to share the drafts.
- Thank you. I'm available [times].
